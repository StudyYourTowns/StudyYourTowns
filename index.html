This is a proposal to study consolidation, not to merge the towns.

New Jersey law allows residents to petition for a formal study of whether municipal consolidation makes sense. The study would examine taxes, schools, services, finances and the structure of local government before any decision about actually consolidating the towns is made.

Frequently Asked Questions

The central idea is simple: before deciding whether these communities should consolidate, residents can request a formal study of what consolidation would actually mean.

1. What is this about?

Study Your Towns is a resident-led effort to explore whether municipal consolidation involving Collingswood, Oaklyn and Woodlynne deserves formal study.

New Jersey's Local Option Municipal Consolidation process allows a representative committee of registered voters from two or more contiguous municipalities to petition the New Jersey Local Finance Board for creation of a Municipal Consolidation Study Commission.

2. Does signing a petition merge the towns?

No. Signing the petition does not consolidate the municipalities and does not authorize consolidation.

The petition asks the New Jersey Local Finance Board to create a Municipal Consolidation Study Commission to formally examine whether consolidation is feasible and what its consequences would be.

3. How does the process work?

Under the Local Option process, a representative committee of registered voters collects the signatures required by state law and petitions the New Jersey Local Finance Board.

The application proposes how study commissioners would be selected, their qualifications, the study timetable and other details of the study process.

Public hearings are held in each participating municipality, along with a joint public hearing accessible to residents of all participating municipalities.

If the Local Finance Board approves the application, the Municipal Consolidation Study Commission is created and conducts the formal study.

4. How many petition signatures are needed?

Under New Jersey's Local Option Municipal Consolidation law, a resident petition must contain signatures from registered and qualified voters equal to at least 10% of the total votes cast in the participating municipalities at the most recent general election at which members of the New Jersey General Assembly were elected.

The precise petition requirements and signature calculation should be confirmed with the New Jersey Division of Local Government Services before petitions are circulated.

5. What would the study examine?

A formal study can examine issues including:

  • municipal and school property taxes
  • state aid
  • municipal and school debt
  • government administration
  • schools and educational services
  • employees and labor obligations
  • public works and other municipal services
  • facilities and equipment
  • existing shared-service arrangements
  • potential costs and savings of consolidation

The purpose is to examine potential advantages, disadvantages, costs and savings before any consolidation plan is considered for approval.

6. Could consolidation lower my property taxes?

Possibly, but that is one of the central questions the formal fiscal analysis would need to answer.

Consolidation could reduce some duplicated administrative expenses. At the same time, tax rates could be affected by differences in property values, existing debt, school finances, state aid and the costs of combining operations.

The New Jersey Department of Community Affairs is required to prepare an objective fiscal study for the commission.

7. What about our schools?

The three communities already have important educational relationships. Oaklyn students attend Collingswood beginning in the middle-school grades, while Woodlynne students attend Collingswood High School through existing sending-receiving arrangements.

A consolidation study could examine whether a more unified school structure would make educational or financial sense, including effects on administration, curriculum, staffing, special education, transportation, facilities, state aid and school taxes.

If consolidation of the boards of education is included in the study, state law provides for a representative appointed by the Commissioner of Education to serve as a non-voting member of the commission, and the county superintendent of schools conducts a study of the impact on the educational system and its finances.

8. Would our town identities disappear?

A governmental consolidation does not automatically erase the history, neighborhood identity, traditions or community organizations associated with the existing towns.

The Local Option law provides flexibility for a consolidation study to consider issues such as service districts, advisory planning districts and continued geographic references to the former municipalities.

9. Would municipal services be reduced?

That cannot be determined before the study.

A consolidation analysis would examine how services are currently delivered, what staffing and facilities are required, where duplication exists, and whether services could be delivered differently under a consolidated government.

The relevant question is what structure can provide residents with an appropriate level of service at a sustainable cost.

10. What about police, public works and other departments?

All major municipal functions would have to be considered as part of a serious consolidation study.

The study could examine staffing, contracts, facilities, equipment, management, service levels, costs and transition requirements before recommending any particular structure.

11. How long would the study take?

The Local Option process is designed to allow the study timetable to be developed as part of the application rather than imposing a single timetable on every consolidation study.

The application must specify key events and deadlines. State law permits the study period to be as long as three years and requires time for State agencies to review the commission's report.

The actual timetable proposed for Collingswood, Oaklyn and Woodlynne would be established in the application submitted to the Local Finance Board.

12. What happens when the study is finished?

The commission issues its findings and recommendations. A study recommending consolidation does not, by itself, merge the municipalities.

The original application must specify how any proposed consolidation plan would ultimately be approved. New Jersey's Local Option law allows that approval process to involve a voter referendum, approval by the municipal governing bodies, or both.

That means the rules for any eventual decision about actual consolidation are established as part of the study process rather than being decided only after the study is complete.

13. Why these three towns?

Collingswood, Oaklyn and Woodlynne are neighboring Camden County communities with existing connections, including established relationships among their school systems.

Whether those connections are sufficient to make municipal consolidation practical is precisely the type of question a formal study could examine.

14. Does supporting the study mean supporting consolidation?

No.

A resident can believe consolidation deserves serious investigation without having decided whether consolidation itself is desirable.

The study process is intended to produce information residents and local officials can use when considering that later decision.

15. Who is behind Study Your Towns?

Study Your Towns is an independent resident-led project. It is not an official project of Collingswood, Oaklyn, Woodlynne, their school districts, Camden County or the State of New Jersey.

Its purpose is to provide information about the consolidation-study process and encourage public examination of the issue.

Questions or interested in getting involved? studyourtowns@gmail.com

16. Where can I read the law myself?

The process described on this website is the Local Option Municipal Consolidation process contained in N.J.S.A. 40A:65-25 through 40A:65-29.

The New Jersey Department of Community Affairs publishes the Uniform Shared Services and Consolidation Act, which contains these provisions.

How the Local Option Study Process Works

New Jersey's Local Option process allows residents to petition the Local Finance Board directly for creation of a Municipal Consolidation Study Commission. The petition does not itself merge the municipalities.

1

Petition

A representative committee of registered voters collects the signatures required by New Jersey law.

2

Apply to the State

The committee petitions the New Jersey Local Finance Board to create a Municipal Consolidation Study Commission and proposes how the study would be conducted.

3

Public Hearings

Public hearings are held in each participating municipality and at a joint hearing before the Local Finance Board acts on the application.

4

Study

If approved, the commission studies finances, taxes, schools, debt, state aid, employees, services, facilities and other consequences of consolidation.

5

Results

The commission publishes its findings and any proposed consolidation plan. Any actual consolidation would then have to be approved through the method specified in the approved application.

What does signing the petition actually do?

It asks the New Jersey Local Finance Board to authorize a formal study of whether consolidating Collingswood, Oaklyn and Woodlynne makes sense.

It does not merge the towns. It does not approve a consolidation plan. It begins the process of examining the actual numbers and consequences.

Could one municipal government cost less than three? How would property taxes change in each town? Could school administration or facilities be organized differently? What would happen to state aid and existing debt? Would services improve, decline or remain essentially the same?

The Municipal Consolidation Study Commission would be created to investigate questions like these.

Signing the petition means supporting a formal study, not committing to consolidation.
