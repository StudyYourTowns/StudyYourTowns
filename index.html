Frequently Asked Questions

The central idea is simple: before deciding whether these communities should consolidate, residents can ask for a formal study of what consolidation would actually mean.

1. What is this about?

Study Your Towns is a resident-led effort to explore whether municipal consolidation involving Collingswood, Oaklyn and Woodlynne deserves formal study.

The question is whether some of the governmental and school structures currently maintained separately could potentially be organized more efficiently while maintaining or improving services.

2. Does signing a petition merge the towns?

No. The petition itself does not consolidate the municipalities.

It begins a statutory process through which residents can vote on whether a Municipal Consolidation Study Commission should be established.

The purpose of that commission is to investigate the consequences of consolidation before residents are asked to make a final decision.

3. How does the process work?

The process begins with petitions signed by registered voters in each participating municipality.

If the statutory petition requirements are satisfied, voters consider whether to establish a consolidation study commission. If approved in the participating municipalities, the commission conducts a formal analysis and ultimately reports its findings and recommendations.

4. How many petition signatures are needed?

Under the resident-initiated process described in New Jersey's Municipal Consolidation Act, the petition requirement is based on 10% of the votes cast in each municipality at the most recent General Assembly election.

The precise number should be verified with the appropriate election and state officials before petitions are circulated.

5. What would the study examine?

A formal study can examine issues including:

  • municipal and school property taxes
  • state aid
  • municipal and school debt
  • government administration
  • schools and educational services
  • employees and labor obligations
  • public works and other municipal services
  • facilities and equipment
  • existing shared-service arrangements
  • potential costs and savings of consolidation

The point is to examine both potential advantages and potential disadvantages.

6. Could consolidation lower my property taxes?

Possibly, but that is one of the central questions a formal fiscal analysis would need to answer.

Consolidation could reduce some duplicated administrative expenses. At the same time, tax rates could be affected by differences in property values, existing debt, school finances, state aid and the costs of combining operations.

A study would allow residents to evaluate those numbers rather than relying on assumptions.

7. What about our schools?

The three communities already have important educational relationships. Oaklyn students attend Collingswood beginning in the middle-school grades, while Woodlynne students attend Collingswood High School through existing sending-receiving arrangements.

A consolidation study could examine whether a more unified school structure would make educational or financial sense, including effects on administration, curriculum, staffing, special education, transportation, facilities, state aid and school taxes.

It would also identify potential disadvantages or transition costs.

8. Would our town identities disappear?

A governmental consolidation does not automatically erase the history, neighborhood identity, traditions or community organizations associated with the existing towns.

How a consolidated municipality would be named and structured would be among the issues that would need to be addressed during the process.

9. Would municipal services be reduced?

That cannot be determined before the study.

A consolidation analysis would examine how services are currently delivered, what staffing and facilities are required, where duplication exists, and whether services could be delivered differently under a consolidated government.

The relevant question is not simply how much government can be eliminated, but what structure can provide residents with the appropriate level of service at a sustainable cost.

10. What about police, public works and other departments?

All major municipal functions would have to be considered as part of a serious consolidation study.

The study would examine staffing, contracts, facilities, equipment, management, service levels, costs and transition requirements before recommending any particular structure.

11. How long would this take?

Municipal consolidation is deliberately a multi-stage process rather than an immediate change.

There is first a petition process, followed by an election on whether to establish a study commission. If voters authorize the study, the commission then conducts its analysis and prepares findings and recommendations.

Any eventual consolidation would require additional formal steps after the study.

12. What happens if the study says consolidation is a bad idea?

That would itself be useful information.

The purpose of studying consolidation is not to predetermine the result. A credible study should identify costs, disadvantages and risks as well as potential savings or improvements.

13. Why these three towns?

Collingswood, Oaklyn and Woodlynne are neighboring Camden County communities with existing connections, including established relationships among their school systems.

Whether those connections are sufficient to make municipal consolidation practical is precisely the type of question a formal study could examine.

14. Does supporting the study mean supporting consolidation?

No.

A resident can believe consolidation deserves serious investigation without having decided whether consolidation itself is desirable.

The study process is intended to produce the information needed to make that later decision.

15. Who is behind Study Your Towns?

Study Your Towns is an independent resident-led project. It is not an official project of Collingswood, Oaklyn, Woodlynne, their school districts, Camden County or the State of New Jersey.

Its purpose is to provide information about the consolidation-study process and encourage public examination of the issue.

How the Study Process Works

1

Petition

Residents collect the number of valid signatures required by New Jersey law in each participating municipality.

2

Vote to Study

If the petitions qualify, residents vote on whether to establish a Municipal Consolidation Study Commission.

3

Study

The commission examines finances, schools, services, debt, state aid, employees, facilities and other consequences of consolidation.

4

Results

The commission publishes its findings and recommendations so residents can evaluate whether consolidation makes sense.

Why sign a petition just to study it?

Because the important questions cannot be answered reliably without examining the actual numbers.

Could one municipal government cost less than three? Could school administration or facilities be organized differently? How would property taxes change in each town? What would happen to state aid and existing debt? Would services improve, decline or remain essentially the same?

A formal study provides a process for answering those questions.

Signing a study petition is not a vote for consolidation. It is a request to find out whether consolidation makes sense.
